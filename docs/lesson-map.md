## Lesson 1: The first sentence

- Source: Arabic textbook PDF pp. 6–12; English key PDF pp. 3–4.
- Concepts and assessment objectives: Make a nominal sentence; distinguish what/who and yes/no questions.
- Rules: هَذَا introduces a nearby masculine thing. كِتَابٌ means “a book”; the final ـٌ is tanwīn, often signalling an indefinite noun. أَ placed before a statement asks a yes/no question. مَا asks “what?”, مَنْ asks “who?”, نَعَمْ means yes, and لَا means no. Start by learning to recognise these sentence shapes; you do not need to memorise technical terms today.
- Vocabulary sample: هَذَا — this (masculine); قَلَمٌ — a pen; مَا — what?.
- Original example type: هَذَا كِتَابٌ — This is a book.
- Connection: No prior grammar is needed. First recognise letters and short vowels.
- Common error: Do not insert a separate word for “is” in these simple present statements.

## Lesson 2: Point a little farther

- Source: Arabic textbook PDF pp. 13–14; English key PDF pp. 5.
- Concepts and assessment objectives: Contrast near/far; join two statements with وَ.
- Rules: ذَلِكَ points to a masculine singular thing farther away. وَ means “and” and attaches to the following word in writing. Compare هَذَا (“this”) with ذَلِكَ (“that”). Neither needs an English-style “is” word in a simple present nominal sentence.
- Vocabulary sample: ذَلِكَ — that (masculine); وَ — and; بَابٌ — a door.
- Original example type: ذَلِكَ قَلَمٌ — That is a pen.
- Connection: Keep the two-word sentence from Day 1 and change only the distance.
- Common error: ذَلِكَ is far, not a second word for nearby “this”.

## Lesson 3: A book or the book?

- Source: Arabic textbook PDF pp. 15–21; English key PDF pp. 6–7.
- Concepts and assessment objectives: Use الْـ; read sun/moon letters; distinguish a definite subject and predicate.
- Rules: Adding الْـ makes a noun definite: كِتَابٌ → الْكِتَابُ. The tanwīn normally disappears when الْـ is added. With sun letters, the ل of الْـ is written but assimilates in speech: الشَّمْسُ is ash-shamsu. With moon letters it is pronounced: الْقَمَرُ is al-qamaru. A definite subject plus an indefinite predicate forms a statement: الْبَيْتُ كَبِيرٌ, “The house is big.”
- Vocabulary sample: الْبَيْتُ — the house; الشَّمْسُ — the sun; الْقَمَرُ — the moon.
- Original example type: الْكِتَابُ — The book
- Connection: Day 1’s indefinite noun now becomes something the listener can identify.
- Common error: Do not retain tanwīn after adding الْـ. Pronunciation assimilation does not delete written ل.

## Lesson 4: Where is it?

- Source: Arabic textbook PDF pp. 22–27; English key PDF pp. 8–11.
- Concepts and assessment objectives: Use location and direction; explain genitive endings; recognise basic pronouns and went/out.
- Rules: أَيْنَ means “where”. Prepositions such as فِي (in), عَلَى (on) and مِنْ (from) are followed by a genitive noun, often with ـِ. إِلَى means “to”. هُوَ refers to a masculine noun and هِيَ to a feminine noun, including things. أَنَا means I and أَنْتَ means you (one male). ذَهَبَ means he went and خَرَجَ means he went out. Proper names can have tanwīn without meaning “a”: بِلَالٌ is simply Bilal.
- Vocabulary sample: فِي — in; عَلَى — on; إِلَى — to.
- Original example type: أَيْنَ الْقَلَمُ؟ — Where is the pen?
- Connection: A known noun can now be located using a preposition.
- Common error: فِي changes the following noun’s case; it does not make the whole sentence feminine.

## Lesson 5: Whose book is it?

- Source: Arabic textbook PDF pp. 28–33; English key PDF pp. 12–13.
- Concepts and assessment objectives: Build iḍāfah; identify both terms; recognise under and the vocative.
- Rules: The iḍāfah construction joins two nouns: the owned thing first, the owner second. The first noun normally loses tanwīn; the second is genitive. The first term takes neither الْـ nor tanwīn in this basic construction; its case depends on its role in the larger sentence. تَحْتَ (“under”) also has a genitive complement. يَا is used to call someone: يَا بِلَالُ. With a definite owner, the whole iḍāfah is definite.
- Vocabulary sample: بَابٌ — a door; اسْمٌ — a name; تَحْتَ — under.
- Original example type: كِتَابُ مُحَمَّدٍ — Muhammad’s book
- Connection: Combine Day 3 definiteness and Day 4 genitive case to express ownership.
- Common error: Do not add الْـ or tanwīn to the first term in the basic ownership construction.

## Lesson 6: This, but feminine

- Source: Arabic textbook PDF pp. 34–39; English key PDF pp. 14–16.
- Concepts and assessment objectives: Use هَذِهِ; form common feminine words; express belonging with لِـ.
- Rules: Use هَذَا with a masculine singular noun and هَذِهِ with a feminine singular noun. Many feminine nouns end in ة, but not all do. لِـ means “for / belonging to”: لِلطَّالِبِ means “belonging to the student”. لِمَنْ means “whose / belonging to whom?”. Learn gender with vocabulary: يَدٌ, a hand, is feminine even without ة. أَيْضًا means also, and جِدًّا means very.
- Vocabulary sample: هَذِهِ — this (feminine); يَدٌ — a hand; لِمَنْ — belonging to whom?.
- Original example type: هَذِهِ سَيَّارَةٌ — This is a car.
- Connection: Keep Day 1’s sentence pattern and match its demonstrative to gender.
- Common error: Not every feminine noun ends in ة: يَدٌ is feminine.

## Lesson 7: Near and far

- Source: Arabic textbook PDF pp. 40–42; English key PDF pp. 17.
- Concepts and assessment objectives: Choose between the four singular near/far demonstratives.
- Rules: هَذَا and هَذِهِ are near demonstratives. ذَلِكَ is the distant masculine form; تِلْكَ is the distant feminine form.
- Vocabulary sample: تِلْكَ — that (feminine); مَدْرَسَةٌ — a school; مُمَرِّضَةٌ — a nurse.
- Original example type: ذَلِكَ بَيْتٌ — That is a house.
- Connection: Put Day 2 distance together with Day 6 gender.
- Common error: Do not use masculine ذَلِكَ with feminine مَدْرَسَةٌ.

## Lesson 8: This book, this sentence

- Source: Arabic textbook PDF pp. 43–47; English key PDF pp. 18–19.
- Concepts and assessment objectives: Separate a noun phrase from a full sentence; use front/behind.
- Rules: هَذَا قَلَمٌ is a complete sentence: “This is a pen.” هَذَا الْقَلَمُ means “this pen”; add a predicate to finish the thought. In هَذَا الْقَلَمُ جَدِيدٌ, جَدِيدٌ supplies the new information. أَمَامَ means in front of; خَلْفَ means behind. The following noun is genitive because it completes an iḍāfah.
- Vocabulary sample: أَمَامَ — in front of; خَلْفَ — behind; جَدِيدٌ — new.
- Original example type: هَذَا الْقَلَمُ جَدِيدٌ — This pen is new.
- Connection: Use the definite article from Day 3 after a demonstrative.
- Common error: هَذَا الْقَلَمُ alone names “this pen”; a predicate is still needed.

## Lesson 9: Describe a noun

- Source: Arabic textbook PDF pp. 48–54; English key PDF pp. 20–23.
- Concepts and assessment objectives: Match adjective and noun; distinguish description/predicate; recognise الَّذِي.
- Rules: The adjective generally follows its noun and agrees with it in definiteness, gender, number and case. Compare بَيْتٌ كَبِيرٌ with الْبَيْتُ الْكَبِيرُ. الَّذِي means “who / which” referring to a masculine singular noun: الْبَيْتُ الَّذِي أَمَامَ الْمَسْجِدِ, “the house which is in front of the mosque”. مَعَ means “with” and has a genitive complement. Adjectives such as كَسْلَانُ (“lazy”) do not take tanwīn; the diptote chapters explain this later.
- Vocabulary sample: كَبِيرٌ — big; صَغِيرٌ — small; الَّذِي — who/which (masculine).
- Original example type: الْبَيْتُ الْكَبِيرُ — The big house
- Connection: Now extend Day 8 noun phrases using agreeing descriptions.
- Common error: الْبَيْتُ كَبِيرٌ says “the house is big”; الْبَيْتُ الْكَبِيرُ names “the big house”.

## Lesson 10: Mine, yours, with me

- Source: Arabic textbook PDF pp. 55–61; English key PDF pp. 24–26.
- Concepts and assessment objectives: Use possessive suffixes, have/with, and basic first/second-person went forms.
- Rules: كِتَابِي means my book; كِتَابُكَ means your book (addressing a male); كِتَابُهُ means his book. عِنْدِي means “I have / with me” in many contexts. كِتَابُهَا means her book. أَبُوكَ (“your father”) and أَخُوكَ (“your brother”) have a special long ū in this nominative construction, unlike أَبِي and أَخِي. ذَهَبْتَ means you (male) went; ذَهَبْتُ means I went. لِي means “I have / belonging to me”; use it for relationships, as in لِي أَخٌ. مَعَ means with, عِنْدَ can refer to being at someone’s place, and مَا can negate: مَا عِنْدِي قَلَمٌ.
- Vocabulary sample: كِتَابِي — my book; عِنْدِي — I have / at my place; مَعَ — with.
- Original example type: كِتَابِي — My book
- Connection: A pronoun can be the second term of the ownership unit from Day 5.
- Common error: كِتَابُكَ is addressing a male; كِتَابُهُ speaks about him.

## Lesson 11: Read about a home

- Source: Arabic textbook PDF pp. 62–63; English key PDF pp. 27–28.
- Concepts and assessment objectives: Trace reference in فِيهِ/فِيهَا; recognise أُحِبُّ and its object.
- Rules: Read this short original passage: بَيْتِي قَرِيبٌ مِنَ الْمَسْجِدِ. فِيهِ كِتَابٌ وَقَلَمٌ. أُحِبُّ بَيْتِي. The suffix ـِي means “my”. فِيهِ means “in it” for a masculine noun such as بَيْتٌ; فِيهَا refers back to a feminine noun. أُحِبُّ means “I love / like”. A direct object is accusative; with “my” attached, that ending is not visible.
- Vocabulary sample: فِيهِ — in him/it (masculine); فِيهَا — in her/it (feminine); أُحِبُّ — I love.
- Original example type: بَيْتِي قَرِيبٌ مِنَ الْمَسْجِدِ — My house is near the mosque.
- Connection: Use pronouns and ownership in connected reading rather than isolated phrases.
- Common error: Choose the suffix from the noun referred to, not from the English word “it”.

## Lesson 12: Speaking to her

- Source: Arabic textbook PDF pp. 64–68; English key PDF pp. 29–30.
- Concepts and assessment objectives: Use أَنْتِ and ـكِ; distinguish she went/you went; use الَّتِي.
- Rules: أَنْتِ addresses one female; أَنْتَ addresses one male. Their possessive endings are ـكِ and ـكَ. ذَهَبَتْ means “she went”, while ذَهَبْتِ means “you (female) went”. These differ in both syllables and meaning, so read the whole form. الَّتِي is the feminine singular relative pronoun, “who / which”; it connects a feminine noun to its description.
- Vocabulary sample: أَنْتِ — you (one female); ذَهَبَتْ — she went; الَّتِي — who/which (feminine).
- Original example type: أَنْتِ طَالِبَةٌ — You are a female student.
- Connection: Compare Day 10’s male addressee with a female addressee.
- Common error: ذَهَبَتْ (dhahabat) and ذَهَبْتِ (dhahabti) address different people.

## Lesson 13: Make it plural

- Source: Arabic textbook PDF pp. 69–82; English key PDF pp. 31–36.
- Concepts and assessment objectives: Recognise plural types, human demonstratives, third-person plural pronouns and verbs.
- Rules: A broken plural changes the singular pattern: كِتَابٌ → كُتُبٌ. Sound plurals add an ending: مُسْلِمٌ → مُسْلِمُونَ in the nominative. هَؤُلَاءِ is “these” for people of either gender; أُولَئِكَ is “those”. هُمْ means they (male group), هُنَّ they (female group); ـهُمْ and ـهُنَّ are the corresponding possessive suffixes. ذَهَبُوا means they (male group) went, while ذَهَبْنَ means they (female group) went. Learn the extra syllables, not only a tiny final mark.
- Vocabulary sample: هَؤُلَاءِ — these (people); هُمْ — they (male group); كُتُبٌ — books.
- Original example type: كُتُبٌ — Books
- Connection: Move from one person to groups; keep agreement and reference in view.
- Common error: Do not assume every plural is formed by adding ون.

## Lesson 14: We and they

- Source: Arabic textbook PDF pp. 83–87; English key PDF pp. 37–39.
- Concepts and assessment objectives: Use نحن/أنتم and نا/كم; contrast plural went forms; interpret which and iḍāfah descriptions.
- Rules: نَحْنُ means we, هُمْ means they (masculine/mixed group), هُنَّ means they (feminine group), and أَنْتُمْ means you (masculine/mixed plural). أَنْتُمْ addresses a male or mixed group; ـكُمْ means your, and ـنَا means our. ذَهَبْتُمْ means you (group) went, while ذَهَبْنَا means we went. An adjective describing a definite iḍāfah follows the completed phrase: بَيْتُ الْمُدَرِّسِ الْجَدِيدُ, “the teacher’s new house”. أَيُّ means which and changes case with its role; مِنْ أَيِّ بَلَدٍ؟ asks “from which country?”. Many foreign names are diptotes, but short names such as نُوحٌ can take tanwīn.
- Vocabulary sample: نَحْنُ — we; أَنْتُمْ — you (male/mixed group); أَيُّ — which?.
- Original example type: نَحْنُ طُلَّابٌ — We are students.
- Connection: Day 13 described a group; now speak as a group or to a group.
- Common error: ذَهَبْنَا (we went) has long ā; ذَهَبْنَ (they, women, went) has short a.

## Lesson 15: Speaking to a group

- Source: Arabic textbook PDF pp. 88–92; English key PDF pp. 40–41.
- Concepts and assessment objectives: Use أنتن and كن; recognise female-group went; apply before/after genitives.
- Rules: أَنْتُنَّ is feminine plural “you”; هُنَّ is feminine plural “they”; ـكُنَّ means “your” when addressing a female group. Contrast these with أَنْتُمْ, هُمْ and ـكُمْ. ذَهَبْتُنَّ means you (female group) went. قَبْلَ means before and بَعْدَ after; their following noun is genitive as the second term of iḍāfah: بَعْدَ الدَّرْسِ. رَجَعَ means he returned.
- Vocabulary sample: أَنْتُنَّ — you (female group); قَبْلَ — before; بَعْدَ — after.
- Original example type: مَنْ أَنْتُنَّ؟ — Who are you (women)?
- Connection: Compare Day 14 male/mixed group forms with a female group.
- Common error: هُنَّ reports about women; أَنْتُنَّ speaks directly to them.

## Lesson 16: The surprising plural rule

- Source: Arabic textbook PDF pp. 93–96; English key PDF pp. 42–43.
- Concepts and assessment objectives: Distinguish human/non-human agreement; recognise further broken plurals.
- Rules: With a non-rational plural such as كُتُبٌ, the adjective or pronoun is commonly feminine singular: الْكُتُبُ جَدِيدَةٌ. Human plurals generally follow different agreement rules.
- Vocabulary sample: جَدِيدَةٌ — new (feminine); مَسَاجِدُ — mosques; بُيُوتٌ — houses.
- Original example type: الْكُتُبُ جَدِيدَةٌ — The books are new.
- Connection: Use plural vocabulary from Day 13 and feminine agreement from Day 6.
- Common error: A plural of things is still plural in meaning even when its agreement is feminine singular.

## Lesson 17: Read the plural correctly

- Source: Arabic textbook PDF pp. 97–100; English key PDF pp. 44.
- Concepts and assessment objectives: Read non-human plural descriptions fluently and compare human groups.
- Rules: This chapter continues Lesson 16 rather than adding a new construction. Read: هَذِهِ الْبُيُوتُ كَبِيرَةٌ. هِيَ قَرِيبَةٌ مِنَ الْمَدْرَسَةِ. The houses are plural in meaning, but هَذِهِ, هِيَ and كَبِيرَةٌ follow the beginner feminine-singular agreement pattern. Compare human students: هَؤُلَاءِ طُلَّابٌ. هُمْ مُجْتَهِدُونَ.
- Vocabulary sample: شَرِكَةٌ — a company; رَخِيصٌ — cheap; قَمِيصٌ — a shirt.
- Original example type: هَذِهِ الْبُيُوتُ كَبِيرَةٌ — These houses are big.
- Connection: Revisit Day 16; this chapter adds practice, not a new construction.
- Common error: Do not use هَؤُلَاءِ for ordinary non-human plurals in this beginner pattern.

## Lesson 18: One, two and more

- Source: Arabic textbook PDF pp. 101–105; English key PDF pp. 45–46.
- Concepts and assessment objectives: Form the nominative dual; use dual demonstratives/pronouns; ask how many.
- Rules: Arabic has singular, dual (exactly two) and plural. The nominative dual ends in ـَانِ: قَلَمٌ → قَلَمَانِ. هَذَانِ points to two masculine things and هَاتَانِ to two feminine things; هُمَا means they two. An adjective agrees in the dual: قَلَمَانِ جَدِيدَانِ. كَمْ asks “how many?” and is followed here by a singular accusative noun: كَمْ كِتَابًا؟. The supporting alif in كِتَابًا is written but not an additional spoken syllable; ة does not take that extra alif: كَمْ سَيَّارَةً؟.
- Vocabulary sample: هَذَانِ — these two (masculine); هُمَا — they two; كَمْ — how many?.
- Original example type: طَالِبَانِ — Two students
- Connection: Add “exactly two” between the singular and plurals you know.
- Common error: كَمْ uses a singular accusative counted noun here, not the plural used after 3–10.

## Lesson 19: Count masculine nouns

- Source: Arabic textbook PDF pp. 106–109; English key PDF pp. 47–48.
- Concepts and assessment objectives: Use 3–10 with masculine singular-gender nouns; explain plural genitive maʿdūd.
- Rules: When counting a masculine singular noun with numbers 3–10, the number normally has the feminine-form ة: ثَلَاثَةُ كُتُبٍ. The counted noun is plural genitive.
- Vocabulary sample: ثَلَاثَةٌ — three (with masculine counted nouns); أَرْبَعَةٌ — four; ثَمَنٌ — price.
- Original example type: ثَلَاثَةُ كُتُبٍ — Three books
- Connection: Combine Day 18 counting with Day 5 iḍāfah.
- Common error: Choose the number form using the singular noun’s gender, not the shape of its plural.

## Lesson 20: Count feminine nouns

- Source: Arabic textbook PDF pp. 110–114; English key PDF pp. 49–50.
- Concepts and assessment objectives: Contrast number forms for masculine/feminine counted nouns.
- Rules: For feminine singular nouns, 3–10 generally use the number without ة: ثَلَاثُ سَيَّارَاتٍ. The counted noun remains plural genitive.
- Vocabulary sample: ثَلَاثٌ — three (with feminine counted nouns); غُرْفَةٌ — a room; كَلِمَةٌ — a word.
- Original example type: ثَلَاثُ سَيَّارَاتٍ — Three cars
- Connection: Keep Day 19’s construction and change the singular noun’s gender.
- Common error: For feminine سَيَّارَةٌ use ثَلَاثُ, not ثَلَاثَةُ.

## Lesson 21: Read, connect, explain

- Source: Arabic textbook PDF pp. 115–117; English key PDF pp. 51.
- Concepts and assessment objectives: Apply earlier grammar in a cumulative reading; recognise we love.
- Rules: This is a consolidation chapter, not a lesson on clock time. Read this original passage: مَدْرَسَتُنَا كَبِيرَةٌ. فِيهَا ثَلَاثَةُ فُصُولٍ. هَذِهِ كُتُبُ الطُّلَّابِ. نَحْنُ نُحِبُّ مَدْرَسَتَنَا. Identify the possessive suffix, location, number construction and non-human plural agreement separately. نُحِبُّ means “we love”.
- Vocabulary sample: نُحِبُّ — we love; لَوْنٌ — a colour; وَاسِعٌ — spacious.
- Original example type: مَدْرَسَتُنَا كَبِيرَةٌ — Our school is big.
- Connection: Return to connected reading from Day 11, now with plurals and numbers.
- Common error: Do not search for a new rule in every sentence; often familiar rules are combined.

## Lesson 22: Recognise the diptote

- Source: Arabic textbook PDF pp. 118–120; English key PDF pp. 52–53.
- Concepts and assessment objectives: Distinguish diptotes from ordinary nouns and from context-driven loss of tanwīn.
- Rules: An ordinary noun can take tanwīn when no other rule removes it: كِتَابٌ. A diptote, الْمَمْنُوعُ مِنَ الصَّرْفِ, does not take tanwīn. Book 1 groups include many feminine names, masculine names ending in ة or ان, certain foreign names, أَفْعَلُ names and adjectives, فَعْلَانُ adjectives, and certain broken-plural patterns such as مَسَاجِدُ and مَفَاتِيحُ. Not every name or broken plural is a diptote. Learn a word’s pattern with its vocabulary.
- Vocabulary sample: أَحْمَرُ — red; مَفَاتِيحُ — keys; أَحْمَدُ — Ahmad.
- Original example type: أَحْمَدُ طَالِبٌ — Ahmad is a student.
- Connection: Collect the no-tanwīn patterns encountered in names, adjectives and plurals.
- Common error: No tanwīn alone does not prove a word is a diptote: الْـ and iḍāfah also remove it.

## Lesson 23: Diptotes in the genitive

- Source: Arabic textbook PDF pp. 121–124; English key PDF pp. 54.
- Concepts and assessment objectives: Explain diptote genitive fatḥah in prepositional and ownership contexts.
- Rules: A preposition still makes its noun genitive. Ordinary triptotes normally show kasrah: مِنْ بِلَالٍ. A diptote without الْـ and not serving as the first term of an iḍāfah shows fatḥah instead: مِنْ أَحْمَدَ. The same applies when it is the second term: كِتَابُ أَحْمَدَ. It remains genitive even though the visible sign is ـَ. A diptote with الْـ, or serving as the first term of an iḍāfah, takes kasrah in the genitive: فِي الْمَسَاجِدِ and فِي مَسَاجِدِ الْمَدِينَةِ. This last comparison clarifies the exception; it is not a reason to treat all names alike.
- Vocabulary sample: مِنْ — from; بِلَالٌ — Bilal (triptote); مَسَاجِدُ — mosques (diptote).
- Original example type: مِنْ أَحْمَدَ — From Ahmad
- Connection: Use Day 4’s genitive and Day 5’s possessor with Day 22’s diptotes.
- Common error: Fatḥah is not always accusative; a bare diptote can be genitive with fatḥah.
